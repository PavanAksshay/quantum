# Section 7: Conclusion

In this study, we conducted a rigorous, multi-dataset empirical evaluation to address a foundational question in applied quantum machine learning: *under controlled, strictly matched conditions, do parameter-free quantum fidelity kernels provide a consistent and practically meaningful performance advantage over matched classical Gaussian RBF kernels for text security classification?* By evaluating models across three standardized corpora (SMS Spam Collection, CEAS 2008, and the multi-source MeAJOR archive), controlling for upstream representation dimensions, conducting dimensionality sweeps across $2\text{D}\text{--}12\text{D}$, testing cross-source domain generalization, analyzing induced feature space geometry, and replicating tests across 10 independent random seeds with bootstrap confidence intervals and permutation tests, we established a reproducible empirical benchmark.

Under canonical in-distribution (IID) evaluation, the parameter-free quantum fidelity kernel ($2$-layer cyclic $ZZFeatureMap$) is competitive with the matched classical Gaussian RBF baseline. While the quantum model achieved small, statistically detectable improvements at intermediate dimensions ($+0.46$ percentage points at 8D, $p = 0.0016$; $+0.57$ percentage points at 10D, $p = 0.0052$), both margins fell strictly within the study's predefined practical-equivalence threshold ($\varepsilon = 0.01$ F1). At 12 dimensions, the performance curves converged to complete statistical and practical parity ($\Delta \text{F1} = +0.0014$, $p = 0.2824$, 95% CI $[-0.0010, +0.0037]$).

Systematic dimensionality sweeps across $2\text{D}\text{--}12\text{D}$ revealed that classification performance is primarily governed by representation capacity rather than kernel architecture. Expanding dimensionality from 2D to 12D produced a substantial monotonic gain ($+26.72$ pp for quantum, $+23.88$ pp for RBF), resolving earlier observations of low-dimensional weakness as artifacts of aggressive representation compression via TruncatedSVD rather than intrinsic geometric failures. Furthermore, representation ablation demonstrated that upstream feature geometry dominates kernel choice: switching from TF-IDF to dense RoBERTa embeddings inverted the relative ranking between quantum and classical models by a net margin of $3.90$ percentage points ($+0.95$ pp quantum advantage on TF-IDF versus $-2.95$ pp classical advantage on RoBERTa).

When evaluated under cross-source domain transfer across historical email collections (Direction B: training on TREC 2007 and testing on TREC 2005/2006), the quantum kernel demonstrated no robustness advantage. Instead, the matched classical RBF baseline significantly outperformed the quantum classifier by $2.33$ percentage points ($\Delta \text{F1} = -0.0233$, $p = 0.0046$, Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted $p = 0.0069$), a margin that exceeded the practical-equivalence threshold. While all compressed models suffered from vocabulary drift interacting with low-dimensional projections, the quantum kernel experienced greater degradation than its classical counterpart under distribution shift.

Geometric diagnostics elucidated the structural relationship between quantum and classical feature spaces. Quantum and RBF Gram matrices exhibited moderate-to-strong entrywise correlation ($r \approx 0.55\text{--}0.65$ through 12D), confirming that the unparameterized quantum feature map partially mirrors classical radial decay while maintaining distinct high-dimensional structure. Across all corpora, the quantum kernel exhibited a $50\%\text{--}60\%$ deficit in kernel-target label alignment relative to RBF. Furthermore, within-dataset regressions revealed a strong inverse association between individual state entropy and pairwise kernel diversity ($r = -0.7822$ to $-0.8257$), refuting the heuristic assumption that greater single-state dispersion across Hilbert space automatically confers superior pairwise discriminative power.

From a computational engineering perspective, classical statevector simulation of quantum kernels incurred steep execution overhead as dimensionality increased. At 12 dimensions, computing the quantum kernel required $108.8$ seconds per run compared to $1.7$ seconds for classical RBF—a $\approx 64\times$ execution time penalty for a nominal in-distribution gain of $0.14$ percentage points—while 16-dimensional simulation encountered an unsustainable memory allocation ceiling (>10.5 GB on 10,000 samples).

In conclusion, **under the evaluated conditions, the empirical evidence does not support a consistent or practically meaningful advantage for parameter-free quantum fidelity kernels in text security classification**. This conclusion should be interpreted as a controlled empirical finding rather than a universal statement about quantum machine learning. For production security NLP, classical linear models operating on full vocabulary representations or fine-tuned transformer backbones continue to provide superior accuracy, computational efficiency, and transfer robustness. Ultimately, this work provides a disciplined, reusable benchmarking template—demonstrating that future claims of quantum advantage in text processing must jointly control representation dimensionality, baseline matching, domain shift, statistical replication, and computational cost.
