# Section 1: Introduction

Text-based social engineering attacks, including email phishing, SMS scams, and fraudulent communications, represent one of the most pervasive and economically damaging threat vectors in modern digital infrastructure [Al-Sallami et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2022]. Automated defense mechanisms rely heavily on natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning classifiers to filter malicious content before it reaches end users. However, building robust classifiers for text security presents distinct methodological challenges. Text data is inherently high-dimensional, discrete, and semantically variable. Furthermore, security environments are characterized by persistent distribution shift: adversaries continuously modify lexical patterns to evade detection filters, attack campaigns differ substantially across organizational sources, and seasonal shifts alter background communications [Cova et al., 2008; Verma & Hossain, 2017 [VERIFY CITATION]]. Consequently, text security classifiers that achieve near-perfect in-distribution accuracy frequently suffer severe degradation when deployed out-of-distribution across unseen sender sources or evolving domains [Al-Sallami et al., 2023]. In this context, the choice of feature representation and the geometry of the decision boundary play decisive roles in determining whether a model generalizes reliably or overfits to source-specific lexical artifacts.

In recent years, quantum machine learning (QML) has emerged as an alternative paradigm for non-linear pattern recognition, with quantum kernel methods receiving particular theoretical attention [Havlíček et al., 2019; Schuld & Killoran, 2019]. In a quantum support vector classifier (QSVC), classical input vectors $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are mapped into quantum states $|\psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle$ residing in a $2^n$-dimensional complex Hilbert space via a parameterized unitary circuit $\mathcal{U}_{\Phi(\mathbf{x})}$. Rather than performing explicit optimization in this exponentially large Hilbert space, the model evaluates pairwise quantum state fidelities to construct a kernel matrix, $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = |\langle \psi(\mathbf{x}) | \psi(\mathbf{z}) \rangle|^2$, which is subsequently supplied to a standard dual quadratic program [Cortes & Vapnik, 1995; Havlíček et al., 2019]. Theoretical investigations have established that certain quantum feature maps generate inner products that are classically intractable to estimate efficiently, offering conjectured separations on group-theoretic and engineered data distributions [Huang et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021]. This mathematical framework has motivated the scientific hypothesis that quantum kernels might uncover non-linear structural regularities in natural language representations that remain inaccessible to standard classical kernels, potentially conferring advantages in classification accuracy or robustness under distribution shift.

Driven by this theoretical potential, a growing body of literature has explored quantum classifiers and quantum kernels for cybersecurity tasks, including network intrusion detection, malicious URL identification, and email phishing detection [e.g., Hridi et al., 2026; Guddanti et al., 2026; Shahriyar et al., 2025; Sagingalieva et al., 2022]. Several preliminary studies have reported promising detection accuracies using quantum support vector machines and variational quantum circuits (VQC) [Garg et al., 2024; Shukla et al., 2023]. However, an examination of the empirical literature reveals widespread methodological limitations that prevent firm conclusions regarding true quantum utility. First, prior applied studies have largely relied on very small sample sizes ($N < 500$ to $2,000$ texts) or synthetic toy benchmarks, limiting statistical power [Hridi et al., 2026; Garg et al., 2024]. Second, many experimental designs suffer from subtle data leakage, such as fitting tokenizers, vectorizers, or dimensionality reduction projections across combined training and test partitions prior to splitting. Third, quantum models are frequently evaluated against unmatched classical controls—such as comparing high-capacity quantum kernels against un-regularized or un-tuned linear classifiers operating on disparate feature representations—rather than strictly matched classical non-linear baselines [Shahriyar et al., 2025; Shukla et al., 2023]. Fourth, existing evaluations almost exclusively report in-distribution cross-validation, omitting rigorous cross-source or out-of-distribution domain-shift evaluations essential for security applications [Al-Sallami et al., 2023]. Finally, exact computational costs, including statevector simulation runtimes and memory footprints, are rarely documented alongside reported metrics [Li et al., 2026].

These methodological confounds create an unresolved scientific problem: reported empirical advantages in applied QML literature may easily be artifacts of unmatched baselines, feature pre-selection biases, or unvalidated split partitions, rather than intrinsic properties of quantum Hilbert-space geometry. Furthermore, theoretical research has increasingly demonstrated that generic, unconstrained quantum feature maps suffer from exponential concentration and expressivity bottlenecks as dimensionality grows [Thanasilp et al., 2024; Kübler et al., 2021], while classical learning algorithms remain highly competitive whenever classical training data is abundant [Huang et al., 2021]. Consequently, isolated accuracy figures on single datasets cannot determine whether quantum kernel methods provide a genuine practical advantage for real-world text security. The critical scientific question is: *Under controlled matched conditions—with identical feature representations, matched dimensionality, zero data leakage, and rigorous out-of-distribution evaluation—do quantum kernel methods provide a consistent and practically meaningful advantage over matched classical RBF kernels for text-based scam and phishing detection?*

To resolve this question, we present a controlled multi-dataset empirical benchmark comparing parameter-free quantum fidelity kernels with matched classical radial basis function (RBF) kernels. Our study evaluates three distinct security text corpora comprising over 152,000 text samples: the SMS Spam Collection (5,572 messages), the CEAS 2008 Email Corpus (15,000 canonical samples), and the multi-source MeAJOR archive (108,684 usable emails across TREC 2005, 2006, and 2007). We enforce strict methodological hygiene across all experiments: (1) text is standardized to NLP content with auxiliary metadata stripped; (2) feature extraction (TF-IDF up to 50,000 n-grams) and linear dimensionality reduction (TruncatedSVD + StandardScaler) are fitted strictly on training partitions to ensure zero test leakage; (3) both quantum and classical RBF models receive identical low-dimensional projections ($d \in [2, 16]$ components/qubits); (4) the quantum model utilizes the canonical two-layer cyclic $ZZFeatureMap$ evaluated via exact double-precision fidelity inner products; (5) decision thresholds are optimized strictly on validation data; and (6) all primary comparisons are executed across 10 independent random seeds with non-parametric bootstrap confidence intervals and paired permutation tests under a predefined practical-equivalence criterion ($\varepsilon = 0.01$ F1). In addition to in-distribution scaling, we conduct multi-source domain-holdout evaluations (TREC 2007 $\to$ TREC 2005/2006), feature space geometric diagnostics (kernel-target alignment, Gram matrix entry correlations, state dispersion), and wall-clock computational profiling.

Our empirical findings provide a coherent, multi-faceted answer across all evaluated dimensions:
1. **In-Distribution Equivalence**: On MeAJOR 8D under in-distribution (IID) conditions across 10 independent seeds, the quantum kernel achieves $\text{F1} = 0.8754 \pm 0.0029$, tracking the matched classical RBF kernel ($\text{F1} = 0.8709 \pm 0.0030$). Although the paired difference ($\Delta \text{F1} = +0.0046 \pm 0.0027$; 95% Bootstrap CI $[+0.0030, +0.0061]$) is statistically distinguishable from zero ($p = 0.0016$), it remains strictly within our predefined practical-equivalence boundary ($\varepsilon = 0.01$). Both nonlinear models outperform the matched 8D Linear SVM baseline ($\text{F1} = 0.8445 \pm 0.0022$).
2. **Dimensionality Recovery**: Scaling representation dimensionality from 2D to 12D produces a monotonic $+41.7\%$ relative gain in quantum F1 ($0.6447 \to 0.9137$), closely tracking classical RBF recovery ($0.6735 \to 0.9123$). At 12 dimensions, the performance gap narrows to $\Delta \text{F1} = +0.0014 \pm 0.0040$ with the 95% bootstrap confidence interval $[-0.0010, +0.0037]$ spanning zero ($p = 0.2824$), demonstrating complete practical parity and confirming that low-dimensional weakness ($<8\text{D}$) is an information bottleneck caused by PCA compression rather than an intrinsic failure of the quantum feature map.
3. **Representation Dominance**: Text representation design exerts substantially larger effects than kernel substitution. Switching from dense RoBERTa embeddings to sparse TF-IDF inverted the quantum margin by $3.90$ percentage points on CEAS, while high-dimensional Linear SVM on 50k TF-IDF achieves $\text{F1} = 0.9721$ on MeAJOR, outperforming low-dimensional nonlinear models by $>10$ percentage points.
4. **Geometric Similarity and Label Alignment Deficit**: Quantum Gram matrix entries correlate moderately with classical RBF geometry ($r = 0.55\text{--}0.65$ across 2D–12D), but quantum kernel-target label alignment is $50\%\text{--}60\%$ lower than classical RBF across all three benchmark corpora (e.g., $0.038$ vs $0.069$ on MeAJOR).
5. **Absence of Domain-Shift Robustness**: Under cross-source holdout evaluation (Direction B: training on TREC 2007 and testing on TREC 2005/2006 across 10 seeds), the quantum kernel degrades by $23.7\%$ relative to IID ($\text{F1} = 0.6680 \pm 0.0094$), while classical RBF degrades by $20.6\%$ ($\text{F1} = 0.6913 \pm 0.0161$). The matched RBF kernel significantly outperforms the quantum kernel ($\Delta \text{F1} = -0.0233 \pm 0.0200$; 95% Bootstrap CI $[-0.0353, -0.0117]$; $p = 0.0046$), demonstrating that the evaluated quantum feature map provides no empirical defense against out-of-distribution domain shift.
6. **Computational Simulation Overhead**: In practical simulation workloads, exact quantum statevector evaluation scales rapidly with dimensionality, requiring $108.8\text{s}$ per run at 12D compared to $1.7\text{s}$ for classical RBF ($64\times$ execution time penalty) without delivering measurable predictive gains.

In summary, the primary contribution of this work is empirical rather than algorithmic. We provide: (1) a controlled, leakage-safe multi-dataset benchmark evaluating quantum fidelity kernels against matched classical baselines for text security; (2) an empirical analysis of dimensionality scaling demonstrating information bottleneck recovery up to 12 qubits; (3) the first multi-source domain-holdout evaluation testing quantum kernel transfer robustness in security NLP; (4) a geometric and computational cost analysis connecting kernel alignment, Gram structure, state dispersion, and simulation overhead; and (5) a fully reproducible open-source evaluation suite with frozen data splits and 10-seed inferential testing. 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work in quantum kernels, QML security, and domain robustness; Section 3 details our methodology, datasets, feature extraction, and statistical evaluation protocol; Section 4 presents empirical results across all six research questions; Section 5 discusses theoretical connections, representation dominance, and practical implications; Section 6 outlines limitations and threats to validity; Section 7 describes open-science reproducibility resources; and Section 8 concludes.
